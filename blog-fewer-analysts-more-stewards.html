FEWER ANALYSTS. MORE STEWARDS.

WAREHOUSE ONE · ISSUE 01 Why leaner teams are the real story behind AI agents — not the AI itself.

A few years ago, the playbook for "getting data-driven" was the same at almost every company: hire analysts, build dashboards, wait for reports.

It worked, in the sense that it produced dashboards. Whether it produced answers is a different question — and if you've ever sat in a meeting waiting on "the numbers" from a team that's three requests deep in a backlog, you already know how that one lands.

That model isn't collapsing because AI is trendy. It's collapsing because it was never built for how businesses actually ask questions.

THE DASHBOARD WAS NEVER THE BOTTLENECK. THE QUEUE WAS.

Here's the part that doesn't show up in the AI hype: the problem was never that companies lacked dashboards. Even as more organizations roll out BI tooling, actual usage across employees has stayed remarkably flat — hovering around three in ten.

That gap is the tell. It isn't an adoption problem or a training problem. It's a design problem. A dashboard answers the question someone thought to ask six weeks ago, at the grain someone else decided on, refreshed on someone else's schedule. The moment your actual question is slightly different — which it almost always is — you're not looking at a dashboard anymore. You're filing a ticket.

And once you're filing tickets, headcount becomes the constraint. More questions means more analysts, more SQL, more backlog. Your data team's size ends up tracking your company's curiosity, not its revenue.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY CHANGING

AI agents sitting on top of a real data warehouse break that link. Instead of a fixed set of pre-built views, you get something that can answer the question you actually have, in plain language, against the same governed source of truth every dashboard was supposed to be pulling from anyway.

That's a meaningfully different shape of system. A dashboard is a finite set of questions, answered in advance. An agent is closer to an open line to your data — as long as the ground underneath it is solid. Which is the part almost nobody talks about, and the part that actually decides whether this works.

THE ROLE ISN'T DISAPPEARING. IT'S CHANGING.

None of this means companies stop needing people who understand data. It means the job stops being "write the query" and starts being "own whether the answer is right."

Think of it as the difference between a translator and an editor. The old model needed a person to translate every business question into SQL, one at a time, forever. The new model needs someone to get the underlying definitions right once — what counts as an "active customer," how refunds net against revenue, which timestamp is truth — so that every future question, asked by anyone, in plain English, gets answered consistently.

That's a steward, not an analyst. Fewer people writing one-off queries. More people responsible for the definitions, the data quality, and the judgment calls no model should be making on its own. A smaller team, but a more senior one — spending its time on the questions that genuinely require a human instead of the ones that just required someone who knew SQL.

WHY THIS PRODUCES LEANER TEAMS — NOT JUST FASTER ONES

If you're deciding how to build out a data function this year, this is the part worth sitting with. The economics genuinely change.

▸Old model — cost scales with questions: every new hire, every new team, every "can you pull this real quick" adds to a queue only more analysts can clear. It's linear, it's expensive, and it caps out. Most companies simply stop asking once the wait gets long enough — a quiet tax on every decision they make.
▸New model — cost is front-loaded: you pay for the foundation once — pipelines, warehouse, data models, definitions — and the marginal cost of one more question drops to almost nothing.

That's what lets a five-person data function support an organization that used to need fifteen. Not because AI is cheaper labor, but because the bottleneck it removes was never supposed to scale with headcount in the first place.

THE CATCH — AND IT'S A REAL ONE

An AI agent is only as trustworthy as what it's standing on. Point one at disconnected spreadsheets, undocumented logic, and three "sources of truth" that disagree with each other, and you haven't removed the bottleneck. You've just made the wrong answers arrive faster and more confidently.

This is the real reason implementation matters more than tool choice. The agent layer isn't the hard part anymore. The hard part is still the unglamorous stuff underneath: clean ETL, a warehouse modeled around how your business actually runs, and definitions that are documented instead of living in one analyst's head.

That's the foundation we build before we ever talk about agents. Skip it, and you're not modernizing your data team — you're just automating the confusion.
