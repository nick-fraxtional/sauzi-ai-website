The Data Hire You Needed in 2020 Isn't the One You Need Now

Agents write the pipelines, the models, and the dashboards. What they can't write is what your data means. Meet the role taking its place: the semantic engineer.

The data hire you needed in 2020 isn't the one you need now.

Two pieces published recently — one from dbt, one from dltHub — landed on the same shift from different angles. Agents now write the pipelines, the models, and the dashboards. The part that used to be the job — translating a request into working SQL — is increasingly something a machine does in seconds. What the machine can't do is tell you what your data means. And that, it turns out, is the part that always mattered most.

dltHub gave the emerging role a name: the semantic engineer. dbt described the same person from the inside — the analytics engineer of 2026 as a system designer, a governance owner, and a provider of context to AI. Different labels, one shift.

The Old Model Is Giving Way

Walk into a data org five years ago and you saw the old model in full swing: a 10–15 person team, hand-building pipelines, clearing a queue of tickets, every dashboard a multi-week wait. It worked. It was also slow, expensive, and built on the assumption that humans would do all the building.

The new model looks different:

  • A few strategic people, not fifteen. The team shrinks, and what each person can do climbs with it.
  • AI does the building. Pipelines, transformations, first-draft models — generated, not hand-typed.
  • Humans decide what "good" looks like. What a metric means, what counts as correct, what the agent is and isn't allowed to do.
  • Governance becomes the job, not the afterthought. When agents act on your data, someone has to own the rules they act within.

The Most Valuable Skill Just Changed

For a decade, the question in a data interview was some version of "can you write the query." That skill hasn't become worthless, but it has become abundant — a model can produce it on demand. The scarce skill now is the one no agent can fake: do you actually know what the data represents?

Does a "customer" include the ones who returned everything and never came back? Does "revenue" net out discounts and refunds the way finance reports it? When a subscription pauses, is it still active? The person who can answer those questions — and encode the answers into a structured layer the AI reasons over — is the person who makes everything downstream trustworthy. The SQL was never the hard part. The meaning was.

This Is a Promotion, Not a Threat

It's easy to read "AI writes the pipelines now" as bad news for data people. We read it the opposite way. The work that's disappearing is the work nobody actually enjoyed — the ticket queue, the ad-hoc report grind, the hundredth slightly-different dashboard. What's left is the high-value part: designing the system, defining the meaning, owning the governance, and pointing AI at a foundation solid enough to trust.

That's not a smaller job. It's a bigger one, done by fewer people, with far more riding on each decision. The teams that see it as a promotion — and build the skill to match — are the ones who'll come out ahead.

If AI handled the building, what would you want your best data person spending their time on instead?
