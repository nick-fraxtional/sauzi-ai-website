When AI Gets Your Data Wrong, the Model Is Rarely the Problem

Same model. Same question. Schema alone scored 3 out of 10. An ontology scored 10 out of 10. Structure is the step everyone skips — and the one that decides whether AI works.

Same model. Same question. Schema alone scored 3 out of 10. An ontology scored 10 out of 10.

That benchmark, published recently by the dltHub team, is the clearest proof of something we repeat constantly: when AI gets your data wrong, the model is rarely the problem. The structure underneath it is.

The detail that should stop you is this — in some cases the model returned the right answer for the wrong reason. It happened to land on the correct number without understanding what the number meant. That is the most dangerous failure mode there is, because nothing in the output tells you it's about to be wrong the next time.

Why an LLM on Raw Tables Is Always Guessing

Point a capable model at your raw warehouse tables and ask it a business question. It has to infer what a "customer" is. Whether an "order" includes refunds. Whether a "subscription" that paused last month still counts as active. None of that lives in the column names. It lives in the heads of your team, in tribal knowledge, in the one analyst who knows that status = 3 means churned.

An ontology — or a semantic layer, or well-built dimension and fact tables — writes that knowledge down in a form the machine can use. It tells the AI what a customer, an order, and a subscription actually are, and how they relate. Give the model that, and the same model that scored 3 out of 10 suddenly scores 10. The intelligence didn't change. The context did.

The Whole Industry Just Said the Same Thing

This isn't one vendor's pitch. Over a single week, the biggest names in data converged on the identical point in different words:

  • Google said it plainly: traditional data architectures lack the context agents need across the data estate, which is why those agents come back inaccurate.
  • Snowflake framed the agentic enterprise as turning governed context into action — and argued that securing AI agents starts at the data layer, not the model.
  • dbt called the transformation layer the place where "correct" gets defined for autonomous systems, and renamed the core analytics-engineering skill "context engineering."
  • Cube shipped connectors so its semantic layer can feed context directly to agents.

When the warehouses, the transformation tools, and the hyperscalers all land on "define what your data means or your AI is improvising," that's not a trend. That's the consensus catching up to the practice.

Structure Is the Step Everyone Skips

It's easy to see why. Structuring data is unglamorous. It doesn't demo well. It's the modeling work that happens before anyone gets to type a question and watch an answer appear. So teams skip it, wire an LLM straight to the database, get an impressive demo, and then quietly stop trusting the thing the first time it confidently returns a wrong number.

The fix isn't a smarter model. It's the work underneath: define your entities, build the dimension and fact tables, document what each metric means, and give the AI a structured layer to reason over instead of raw tables to guess at. Do that once, and every question that comes after it gets more reliable.

Structure is the step everyone skips. It's also the step that decides whether any of this actually works.
